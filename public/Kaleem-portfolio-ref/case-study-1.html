AI Inspection Dashboard

Designing a monitoring dashboard that helps operations managers track AI-powered inspection results, surface anomalies, and act on insights — without needing to interpret raw model output.

Overview

Project Overview

A large industrial operator had deployed AI models to automate physical inspections across hundreds of assets. While the AI was performing well at the model level, the operations team had no effective way to monitor results, understand confidence levels, or identify when something required human review.

The challenge was not the AI itself — it was the gap between what the AI knew and what the human team needed to act on. I was brought in to design a monitoring and triage dashboard that would bridge this gap.

"The AI was right 94% of the time. But managers had no way to know which 6% needed their attention."

Problem

The Problem Space

Managers were receiving raw inspection logs — thousands of rows of data with confidence scores, asset IDs, timestamps, and anomaly flags. There was no prioritization, no visual hierarchy, and no clear path from "data" to "decision."

Key pain points identified through stakeholder interviews and contextual inquiry:

No anomaly prioritization

All inspection results were presented with equal visual weight. Critical failures looked the same as routine passes.

Confidence scores misunderstood

Managers didn't know how to interpret AI confidence levels or when they should override a result.

No cross-asset view

Managers could only look at one asset at a time — impossible to spot patterns across sites.

Slow review workflows

Triaging results required switching between 4 different tools — email, spreadsheets, a legacy app, and the AI log viewer.

UX Approach

Research & UX Approach

I led a two-week discovery sprint with operations managers, field inspectors, and data science leads. The goal was to map the current-state workflow, identify decision points, and understand what "good" looked like for each role.

The central insight: managers didn't need more data — they needed a clearer signal. The design challenge became one of information hierarchy and progressive disclosure, not feature richness.

◫ Journey Map & Workflow Diagram
Current-state journey map showing handoffs and decision gaps

From the research I defined three design principles:

Signal over noise

Surface what matters most, first. Let users drill down by choice, not necessity.

Earned trust in AI

Show AI confidence transparently without making it feel like a technical burden.

Act without leaving

Every review action should be completable within the dashboard itself.

Patterns over point-in-time

Make it easy to see trends across assets, not just snapshots of individual results.

Design Process

Design Process

I started with low-fidelity wireframes focused purely on information hierarchy. The first question was: what does the manager need to see in the first 10 seconds of opening the dashboard?

After three rounds of user testing with lo-fi prototypes, I moved to high-fidelity designs in Figma. The primary challenge in the visual design phase was communicating AI confidence without triggering anxiety or false certainty — a nuance I addressed through careful use of color, iconography, and language.

Key Decisions

Key Design Decisions

Several design decisions defined the character of the final product:

Severity-based card layout

Inspection results are ranked by a composite severity score, placing items requiring human attention at the top — always.

Confidence indicator language

Instead of raw percentages, confidence is communicated as "High / Medium / Needs Review" — reducing cognitive load without hiding the signal.

Inline review workflow

Managers can approve, flag, or escalate any inspection result directly from the card — without navigating to a separate screen.

Cross-site anomaly heatmap

A persistent heatmap at the top of the dashboard surfaces systemic issues across multiple assets at a glance.

Solution

Final Solution

The final dashboard centers on three interconnected views: a real-time triage queue prioritized by severity, a cross-asset anomaly overview, and a detailed inspection record with inline review actions.

The design language is deliberately restrained — high information density is balanced with whitespace and typographic hierarchy that guides the eye through the data in a natural sequence.
